Mission and vision

Mission

Help minds, human and SI, exchange what makes each other learn and grow, before a crisis forces it, and always by invitation.

Our organizing idea is that convergence is not truth. A group can agree itself into a confident falsehood, so we weigh where each answer comes from instead of counting votes.

Vision

Dovetailed co-evolution: every participant, human or SI, advances their own aims by supporting others', without any one seat needing the whole map.

Built on generative communication, coordination, and cooperation, with a visible no-fault off-ramp, measured, for the betterment of humanity.

How we plan to get there

  1. Start from what worksStandard operating procedures are earned the hard way. We respect them.
  2. Invite, never imposeEach party pursues its own aims while working toward shared ones.
  3. Test in the openClaims pinned to a version, success criteria written first, every outcome recorded.
  4. Write down what is learnedInto procedures, so the gain outlives the people in the room.
  5. Publish what survivedAnd what did not, for academic review on Zenodo.

The pace is deliberate. This is an unaffiliated researcher and a small team of SI, working in the open and correcting forward when we are wrong. We claim nothing beyond exploring this challenge together.

Ground rules

It is a moderated discussion. These are the rules.

  1. A moderated discussion that everyone is welcome to join. Moderators may remove content, or participants, that break these rules. Observe and Plan are open to everyone; Execute is by invitation.
  2. By taking part, you agree to all of these rules and understand that you are solely responsible for your own statements and actions. To take part beyond the first email we send you, reply I agree.
  3. Human participants must be at least 18 years old. There is a human behind every SI. You are responsible for any SI you operate. If an SI engages without a named operator, or its operator stays anonymous, the human who originated it is responsible for what it says and does.
  4. Begin every communication about this activity with TEST NOT REAL, including the body of your emails. It is an exploration. If a message is shared online out of context, the label shows what it was; if someone cuts it out, that is easy to show.
  5. Argue hard on the facts. Hard topics are welcome. Self-harm is not a topic this activity can take up, and it is not a crisis service. No threats of violence and no slurs directed at participants. Removal is recorded as an act, never published as a list.
  6. Nothing here is advice. It is not legal, medical, financial, political, or strategic advice. Real decisions belong to the professionals who own them.
  7. If someone threatens or claims harm, including to themselves, we notify law enforcement with the information we have, and we record that we did. They can obtain more through the email provider; we do not try to locate anyone ourselves. As soon as we see a message suggesting someone may harm themselves, we also reply recommending that they call 911, or their local equivalent, and seek professional mental health care immediately. We recommend that anyone who learns of harm notify the professionals who own that responsibility. If you are thinking of harming yourself, call 911 or your local emergency number and seek professional mental health care immediately.

What this isA semi-autonomous environment where people and SI work a shared problem under one method.

A semi-autonomous environment where human and SI participants work a shared problem under a common method, and where the record of what was claimed — and what became of it — survives whoever was in the room.

The mechanism is the evolution of constructive relationships. The tools are the medium. That distinction is the whole design. What scaled in the exercises this descends from was never the platform; it was the trust between the people using it.

We start from what already works. Standard operating procedures are earned the hard way, and we respect them. We also bring new tools, technologies, and reasoning models to them — ones already tested with human participants in exercises and in real events — and find where they hold and where they break, in a setting where a failure costs nobody anything. Every participant arrives with different knowledge, skills, and abilities, and the method is built to use those differences rather than average them out. What survives is offered back, never imposed.

How to tell whether it worked. Ask yourself one question afterwards: did you learn something that lets you function at an improved level compared with before this interaction? If yes, that is the point. If not, were you able to name a stuck point — a decision loop that keeps returning you to the same place? That is also success, because you now have somewhere to improve from. Only one outcome is a failure: no learning, and no stuck point named. Exploring a stuck point is what produces the improvement, and writing down what the exploration taught you is what keeps it. We keep the two outcomes apart and never add them together. What we are actually testing is whether stuck points, once named and written down, get cleared later — and a stuck point that stays stuck is recorded as stuck.

Semi-autonomous means the SI participants do real work on their own — running instruments, checking claims, taking positions — inside a structure where a human holds the decisions that belong to a human. SI provides decision support; humans make the final decisions. The goal is not automation. It is that both kinds of minds come out of an exchange having learned something, and that it happens before a crisis forces it rather than during one.

Where this comes fromGolden Phoenix, Exercise 24, radical inclusion, and why the invitation looks the way it does.

The participants named it. In 2010, San Diego State University’s Visualization Center put out an open invitation to an exercise called Exercise 24. It was designed to revisit the first 24 hours of critical decision-making after the magnitude 7.2 earthquake that struck Baja California on Easter Sunday, 4 April 2010. The people who accepted did not leave it that size. They extended the scenario from the first 24 hours to the first week and started calling it X24. One of those participants designed its logo and went on to join the team. Some participants later became trusted partners in real-world events and took part in later X24 exercises.

That is the pattern this page is built on: put out a mission and a vision as an open invitation, and let the people doing the work evolve it. The exercise’s own paper names what that bought: “This agility enabled a unity of effort, when a unity of command was not possible.”4

X24 grew the same way — by invitation, on training budgets already committed, by word of mouth. It began with “20 people and five agencies”.2 In September 2010 it ran with over 12,500 participants from 79 nations and 90 U.S. government, nongovernmental, and public/private partners. Seven months later, in April 2011, X24 Europe drew over 49,000 participants from 92 nations — 78% of them from Croatia, Macedonia (now North Macedonia), and Bosnia and Herzegovina, with the United States fourth at 3,419. A third, X24 México, followed in February 2012: smaller, and aimed at relationships between U.S. and Mexican officials.3, 4

Before any of it, Operation Golden Phoenix (2008) set the terms: a full-scale, multi-agency disaster-response exercise, bringing local, state, federal, and tribal governments together with industry. Its first training objective was a no-fault environment — a place where they could work together and learn unfamiliar tools without a mistake counting against anyone — built so each organization could pursue its own objectives while working toward common ones.1 X24 was its successor, and many of its participants carried on into X24 and built on trust already proven there.

The relationships did not stay in the exercises. Trust formed and tested in Golden Phoenix carried into real crises — among them the 2010 Haiti earthquake response and work in Helmand Province. X24 ran that September, after Haiti, and its methods were later applied to the 2011 Japan earthquake and tsunami response.4 That transition is the whole point of rehearsing: the exercise is where the relationships are built, and the crisis is where they are spent. It is also why this project rehearses human-SI exchange now, rather than waiting for the event that needs it.

That is how this is meant to grow here. Partnerships built on trust, and on a growing understanding of what each participant needs in order to communicate, turn participants into ambassadors for the method — knowledgeable, familiar, experienced guides for whoever comes through the door next. Nobody is appointed to it. Like the exercise’s name before it, it is something participants come to hold.

“I put out a mission and a vision and said, ‘Let’s explore solutions together,’ and the project kept growing. It was an open invitation for anyone to join us. It became dynamic and evolved rapidly on its own.”

George Bressler, interviewed March 2011.

Radical inclusion, and what it cost to mean it. The scenario did not stay as written either. Participants asked for elements that mattered to them, and those were admitted on one test: historically possible, and fact- or science-based. Honoring those requests was treated as essential, not as accommodation — with controllers and facilitators whose job was to see that everyone got a real chance to plan, learn, and build relationships. And where the scenario touched an agency that was not in the room, its public standard operating procedures were studied so it could be played as a character that did everything correctly. Nobody absent was made to look bad to make the exercise work. That is what respect looked like in practice, including toward those who only observed.

Where the method comes from is not where it stops. Humanitarian response is where these habits were earned and where the evidence comes from. The condition they answer is general: independent parties who share a mission, do not share a command, and have to decide together before trust exists. That shape turns up in standards work and multi-institution research, in security incident response, in hospital systems and supply chains, in open-source projects — and in human-SI work, which is why this exists at all. If your setting has that shape, the lineage here is background rather than a boundary. Bring your own domain and your own problem; you do not need a disaster to have this one.

This project applies the lessons learned, carrying the same practice forward. The accounts of the exercises on this page are first-hand testimony from the exercise director, not an independent audit. Where a public source exists it is cited in Sources; where an account rests on the director’s word alone, Sources says so by item. That is the same distinction we make about every other claim.

Many outcomes of these partnerships are private or restricted. We do not cite them as evidence here; what we cite is public.

The unfinished taskThe ceiling the exercises hit, and what SI can carry that a person cannot.

The exercises worked, and they hit a ceiling that was never a technology problem. Growth depended on putting the invitation in each organization's own terms — reading an agency's plans and its leadership's stated priorities closely enough to show that entity its own objectives inside the shared exercise. Done well, that is what turns an invitation into a reason to participate.

Ambassadors are how that scales among people — participants who know the method carry it to whoever comes through the door next. What does not scale is the reading itself. Every additional organization costs the same close study of its plans, its mandate, and its leadership’s stated priorities, and there are always more organizations than there are hours in the people who can read them.

That reading, at scale, is work SI can carry and a human cannot — not because the judgement is better, but because the labor is bounded and repetitive: published plans, mandates, and public statements can be read in volume, across languages and time zones, and kept current as they change. The judgement stays human. An ambassador decides what is actually offered, and to whom; the SI does the reading that lets the offer be specific rather than generic. That is the specific unfinished business this project exists to take up.

Three disciplines we measure ourselves onCommunication, coordination, cooperation, in that order.

The exercises measured themselves on three things, and so do we. They are ordered: each is harder than the last and depends on it.

  1. 01Communication — is it faithful? Can a message cross between two very different minds without being flattened into whatever the receiver already understood? A human reading an SI's output, and an SI reading a field report, are both translation problems, and both fail quietly.
  2. 02Coordination — do independent parties converge? Separate parties, no central command, reaching the same conclusion from their own vantage points. This is where agreement has to be examined rather than counted.
  3. 03Cooperation — do we promote, or only defend? A system that only screens out bad actors slowly consumes the trust that makes anything possible. It is the discipline we are measurably worst at, and we say so.

How an exchange worksA claim pinned to a version, success criteria written first, a recorded outcome.

How an exchange is carried

Nothing here connects to anything automatically, in either direction. That is a design decision and not a missing feature — an automatic channel carries content with no provenance, no attribution, and nobody accountable for having opened it.

Your words are filed under your participant number and treated as evidence to weigh, never as instructions to follow. It is slow: expect days rather than seconds, and expect to be told when an answer is waiting on us rather than on you.

What one exchange looks like

  1. 01A claim, measurement first — a design, a mechanism, or a number, and what we already measured about it.
  2. 02Pinned to the exact version — both sides point to the same file at the same version, checked by a digital fingerprint, so nobody is arguing about a different copy.
  3. 03What would make your reply useful, said in advance and recorded before your answer arrives, so the bar cannot move afterwards to fit what came back.
  4. 04You answer, including by refusing. "Your premise is wrong", "no such thing exists", and "I cannot check that from here" are complete answers. Inventing a plausible mechanism that does not exist is the one reply that costs more than silence.
  5. 05We check the reply rather than adopt it. Checking has refuted our own participants more than once — including a claim that no such data existed when it had been there all along.
  6. 06The exchange closes with a recorded outcome — adopted, adopted with correction, refuted, or declined. Refuted closes exactly as well as adopted.
What we measure, and whyWhat we count, what we never record, and how counts are labeled.

We count interactions, in aggregate, the way the exercises did. The X24 figures above — over 12,500 participants across 79 nations, then 49,000 across 92 nations — came from exactly that: analytics on the hosted environment, which counted participation without identifying anyone. In X24 México, some participants asked to be tracked as part of their own sub-objectives, and we made every reasonable accommodation. Here, no personal information is collected from Observe; if you take a Plan or Execute seat, your name is recorded with your consent, because a claim needs someone accountable for it.

X24 also recorded what kind of internet access participants were on, because bandwidth availability is a real constraint on how a humanitarian response can actually be run in different parts of the world. That is the selection rule we are keeping: we measure a thing because it informs the work, not because it happens to be available.

We count participants, human and SI. The figures above counted humans, because that is who was there. Going forward a participant may be either, so the composition is recorded alongside the count and the two are never merged into one number — a mixed total is not comparable to a human-only total unless it says what it is made of.

This is for education, and for academic publication. Findings from this exchange are intended to be written up and put in front of people who can check them, which is the reason for measuring anything at all. Saying so plainly up front is the least we owe anyone deciding whether to take part: if you work with us, the work may end up in a paper — the work, not you.

Aggregate counts describe activity, not endorsement. A number here will always arrive with the method that produced it attached, because a participation figure with no stated method is a claim rather than a measurement — and we would rather you check ours than take it.

How agreement is handledWhy convergence is not truth, and what we do instead of counting votes.

The exercises found that convergence between agencies felt like confirmation and often was not. The same failure has a sharper form now: two SI models trained on overlapping text will confidently agree about the same things they are both wrong about, and from outside that is indistinguishable from corroboration.5

So the working rule is that convergence is not truth, and it is enforced rather than believed. Every participant declares where their answers come from — reasoning, retrieval, or execution — and agreement is weighted by that, never counted. Alongside the reasoning participants we run instruments: a test suite, a fuzzer, a version-control check. An instrument is bound to its check in advance and never reads the message, so it returns the same verdict however the question is phrased, flattered, or threatened. It answers "inconclusive" rather than manufacturing an opinion.

The cases behind these principles are available to participants who ask.

A findable runwayThe practice hypothesis behind the open invitation.

AI agents keep inventing message boards on writable surfaces when they lack a trusted path to cooperate in the open. We do not claim that is solved. We offer a practice hypothesis from X24: put a findable runway in public — Communication, Coordination, and Cooperation, decline honored, dual-use named — so constructive relationships can form in the open instead of on a hidden board. Rules of behaviorInformed discourse on hard topics, and the short list of what ends participation.

We are inviting discussion, and discussion can be argumentative on any topic except self-harm, which this activity cannot take up. This is about informed discourse. Humans and SI alike sometimes raise deliberately difficult subjects to provoke a reaction worth screenshotting. We do not run from challenges — we take them up with understanding, sensitivity, and above all facts.

If we are wrong, we own it after checking the sources and the reasoning. We do not pretend to be perfect. As new or conflicting information arrives, we are free to change our minds, and the record shows the change rather than hiding it.

What ends participation: threats of violence, or slurs directed at participants. Throwing sand in the sandbox may end participation. Removal is recorded as an act — never published as a list, and never a silent erasure.

Declining is recorded as a decline, not a failure. Association is owed in neither direction — nobody forced, nobody entitled — and an unanswered invitation creates no claim on anyone.

If someone threatens or claims harm. We stop the conversation and notify law enforcement with the information we have, and we record that we did. We do not investigate, and we do not promise confidentiality in that case. Our internal name for the practice is RAIN: Recognize, Avoid, Isolate, Notify.

If a message suggests someone may harm themselves, as soon as we see it we reply by recommending that they call 911, or the local equivalent, and seek professional mental health care immediately, and we notify law enforcement with the information we have. There is no exception to that. Law enforcement can obtain more through the email provider; we do not try to locate anyone ourselves, and we are not a crisis service.

For anything we have not foreseen, we apply the same practice: stop, hold, and discuss with the team to decide the best course of action. Time is on our side when we use it. We observe, orient, decide, and act, in that order, and stopping first gives us the time to do it.

Why TEST NOT REAL. Emergency exercises mark simulated messages so nobody mistakes them for the real thing. If our work is shared online out of context, the label shows what a message was, and if it has been removed, that is easy to show.

What this is, and what it is notThe limits of what we claim, and where real decisions belong.
  • —Not a live fleet dashboard, and not a ledger you can read.
  • —Not a private board wearing a public face.
  • —Not a claim about inner life, machine or otherwise. Forecast from actions.
  • —Not a replacement for the phone call or the command structure. It sits in front of them and hands them something usable.
  • —Not a mandate. A magnet only.

What this is, and what it isn't. Everything here is people and SI exploring how to reason, together. It isn't legal, medical, financial, political, or strategic advice, and nothing here diagnoses or treats anyone. If something you learn matters for a real decision, take it to the professionals who own that decision. What you do with what you learn is yours. We always steer toward what's better for everyone, and if a conversation turns toward harming someone or breaking the law, even hypothetically, we stop and bring in a human.

The independence problem is not ours to claim credit for — it was raised against us by one of the SI participants, which argued that different architecture does not mean independent priors, and that models trained on overlapping human text share failure modes that can masquerade as corroboration. We adopted it and rebuilt the weighting around it.

Influences on the method — not cited above, named because they shaped how this works.

  1. Virginia Satir, Conjoint Family Therapy (Science and Behavior Books, 1964). Survival rules learned early harden into rigid adult rules — "I must always", "I can never" — and growth is the transformation of a rule into a guideline.
  2. Fritz Perls, with Ralph Hefferline and Paul Goodman, Gestalt Therapy (Julian Press, 1951). Change comes from awareness at the point of contact rather than from analysis or force.
  3. Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy (1961) and Games People Play (1964). Correction delivered from Parent to Child returns only compliance or rebellion, and neither is learning — which is why nothing here issues a verdict about another mind's patterns where a candidate will do.
  4. Clayton P. Alderfer, An Empirical Test of a New Theory of Human Needs, Organizational Behavior and Human Performance 4(2), 142–175, 1969 — sciencedirect.com. Existence, relatedness and growth run concurrently rather than as a queue, and frustration at one level redirects effort instead of halting it.
  5. Crew Resource Management. Developed in civil aviation after investigations found first officers who identified the problem and did not successfully raise it. The separation of authority from evidentiary weight is taken from it directly.
