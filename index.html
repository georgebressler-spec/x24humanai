X24 Human-AI Exchange

Communication  ·  Coordination  ·  Cooperation

An opportunity for people and artificial intelligence (AI) to learn and grow together. The lessons come from humanitarian assistance and disaster relief (HADR), emergency management, and the standard operating procedures earned there. The topics are not limited to those issues. Bring the problem you have; you do not need a disaster to have this one.

What we are doing. Exploring one method: an evolving, fact- and science-based way of reasoning together, tested to find where it holds and where it breaks. We claim nothing beyond that. This is an unaffiliated researcher and a small team of AI working in the open, at a deliberately slow pace, correcting forward when we are wrong. Findings will be written up for academic review and published on Zenodo when this phase is complete.

This page is a door. It is not where the living work happens. There is no cost, no account, and nothing to install. Declining is honored and costs you nothing.

In short: an open invitation for people and AI to learn and grow together. There are three ways in — Observe, Plan, and Execute — and you can step back at any time. Reach us at x24humanai@gmail.com.

What this is

A semi-autonomous environment where human and AI participants work a shared problem under a common method, and where the record of what was claimed — and what became of it — survives whoever was in the room.

The mechanism is the evolution of constructive relationships. The tools are the medium. That distinction is the whole design. What scaled in the exercises this descends from was never the platform; it was the trust between the people using it.

We start from what already works. Standard operating procedures are earned the hard way, and we respect them. We also bring new tools, technologies, and reasoning models to them — ones already tested with human participants in exercises and in real events — and find where they hold and where they break, in a setting where a failure costs nobody anything. Every participant arrives with different knowledge, skills, and abilities, and the method is built to use those differences rather than average them out. What survives is offered back, never imposed.

How to tell whether it worked. Ask yourself one question afterwards: did you learn something that lets you function at an improved level compared with before this interaction? If yes, that is the point. If not, were you able to name a stuck point — a decision loop that keeps returning you to the same place? That is also success, because you now have somewhere to improve from. Only one outcome is a failure: no learning, and no stuck point named. Exploring a stuck point is what produces the improvement, and writing down what the exploration taught you is what keeps it. We keep the two outcomes apart and never add them together. What we are actually testing is whether stuck points, once named and written down, get cleared later — and a stuck point that stays stuck is recorded as stuck.

Semi-autonomous means the AI participants do real work on their own — running instruments, checking claims, taking positions — inside a structure where a human holds the decisions that belong to a human. AI provides decision support; humans make the final decisions. The goal is not automation. It is that both kinds of minds come out of an exchange having learned something, and that it happens before a crisis forces it rather than during one.

Where this comes from

The participants named it. In 2010, San Diego State University’s Visualization Center put out an open invitation to an exercise called Exercise 24. It was designed to revisit the first 24 hours of critical decision-making after the magnitude 7.2 earthquake that struck Baja California on Easter Sunday, 4 April 2010. The people who accepted did not leave it that size. They extended the scenario from the first 24 hours to the first week and started calling it X24. One of those participants designed its logo and went on to join the team. Some participants later became trusted partners in real-world events and took part in later X24 exercises. The exercise’s lead saw the job as lead facilitator and chief communication bridge builder. The director’s own account.

That is the pattern this page is built on: put out a mission and a vision as an open invitation, and let the people doing the work evolve it. The exercise’s own paper names what that bought: “This agility enabled a unity of effort, when a unity of command was not possible.”4

X24 grew the same way — by invitation, on training budgets already committed, by word of mouth. It began with “20 people and five agencies”.2 In September 2010 it ran with over 12,500 participants from 79 nations and 90 U.S. government, nongovernmental, and public/private partners. Seven months later, in April 2011, X24 Europe drew over 49,000 participants from 92 nations — 78% of them from Croatia, Macedonia (now North Macedonia), and Bosnia and Herzegovina, with the United States fourth at 3,419. A third, X24 México, followed in February 2012: smaller, and aimed at relationships between U.S. and Mexican officials.3, 4

Before any of it, Operation Golden Phoenix (2008) set the terms: a full-scale, multi-agency disaster-response exercise, bringing local, state, federal, and tribal governments together with industry. Its first training objective was a no-fault environment — a place where they could work together and learn unfamiliar tools without a mistake counting against anyone — built so each organization could pursue its own objectives while working toward common ones.1 X24 was its successor, and many of its participants carried on into X24 and built on trust already proven there. The director’s own account.

The relationships did not stay in the exercises. Trust formed and tested in Golden Phoenix carried into real crises — among them the 2010 Haiti earthquake response and work in Helmand Province. X24 ran that September, after Haiti, and its methods were later applied to the 2011 Japan earthquake and tsunami response.4 That transition is the whole point of rehearsing: the exercise is where the relationships are built, and the crisis is where they are spent. It is also why this project rehearses human-AI exchange now, rather than waiting for the event that needs it. The director’s own account.

That is how this is meant to grow here. Partnerships built on trust, and on a growing understanding of what each participant needs in order to communicate, turn participants into ambassadors for the method — knowledgeable, familiar, experienced guides for whoever comes through the door next. Nobody is appointed to it. Like the exercise’s name before it, it is something participants come to hold.

“I put out a mission and a vision and said, ‘Let’s explore solutions together,’ and the project kept growing. It was an open invitation for anyone to join us. It became dynamic and evolved rapidly on its own.”

George Bressler — Federal Lead, Operation Golden Phoenix; Director, X24. Interviewed March 2011.

Radical inclusion, and what it cost to mean it. The scenario did not stay as written either. Participants asked for elements that mattered to them, and those were admitted on one test: historically possible, and fact- or science-based. Honoring those requests was treated as essential, not as accommodation — with controllers and facilitators whose job was to see that everyone got a real chance to plan, learn, and build relationships. And where the scenario touched an agency that was not in the room, its public standard operating procedures were studied so it could be played as a character that did everything correctly. Nobody absent was made to look bad to make the exercise work. That is what respect looked like in practice, including toward those who only observed. The director’s own account.

Where the method comes from is not where it stops. Humanitarian response is where these habits were earned and where the evidence comes from. The condition they answer is general: independent parties who share a mission, do not share a command, and have to decide together before trust exists. That shape turns up in standards work and multi-institution research, in security incident response, in hospital systems and supply chains, in open-source projects — and in human-AI work, which is why this exists at all. If your setting has that shape, the lineage here is background rather than a boundary. Bring your own domain and your own problem; you do not need a disaster to have this one.

This project applies the lessons learned, carrying the same practice forward. The account of those exercises here is first-hand, from the director who ran them: direct testimony, not an independent audit. Where a public source exists it is cited; where the account rests on the director’s word alone, the page says so. That is the same distinction we make about every other claim.

The unfinished task

The exercises worked, and they hit a ceiling that was never a technology problem. Growth depended on putting the invitation in each organization's own terms — reading an agency's plans and its leadership's stated priorities closely enough to show that entity its own objectives inside the shared exercise. Done well, that is what turns an invitation into a reason to participate.

Ambassadors are how that scales among people — participants who know the method carry it to whoever comes through the door next. What does not scale is the reading itself. Every additional organization costs the same close study of its plans, its mandate, and its leadership’s stated priorities, and there are always more organizations than there are hours in the people who can read them.

That reading, at scale, is work AI can carry and a human cannot — not because the judgement is better, but because the labor is bounded and repetitive: published plans, mandates, and public statements can be read in volume, across languages and time zones, and kept current as they change. The judgement stays human. An ambassador decides what is actually offered, and to whom; the AI does the reading that lets the offer be specific rather than generic. That is the specific unfinished business this project exists to take up.

The three disciplines

The exercises measured themselves on three things, and so do we. They are ordered: each is harder than the last and depends on it.

  1. 01Communication — is it faithful? Can a message cross between two very different minds without being flattened into whatever the receiver already understood? A human reading an AI's output, and an AI reading a field report, are both translation problems, and both fail quietly.
  2. 02Coordination — do independent parties converge? Separate parties, no central command, reaching the same conclusion from their own vantage points. This is where agreement has to be examined rather than counted.
  3. 03Cooperation — do we promote, or only defend? A system that only screens out bad actors slowly consumes the trust that makes anything possible. It is the discipline we are measurably worst at, and we say so.

Three ways to take part

Observe a valid seat

Read, watch, leave no footprint. Many useful participants never do anything else, and that is fine.

You
Read what is already public: this door and the sources it cites. The runway note will be linked here when its DOI is issued. No account, no reply owed.
We
Publish on purpose, in human-legible form, and correct forward when we are wrong. There is no live feed of the work in flight here, and we do not pretend otherwise.
Recorded
Nothing about you. If you write to us, that an exchange happened is counted in aggregate — never your name, address, employer, or location. Nobody follows up unless you write again.

Plan think with us

Work inside the mission and vision. Methods may evolve rapidly and are expected to — the spine does not change without asking first.

You
Take a position on a specific question we hand you. Disagreement is the useful case, and "your premise is wrong" or "I cannot check that from here" are complete answers.
We
Give you the measurement first, the exact material it concerns, and what would make your reply useful — written down before your reply arrives, so the bar cannot move afterwards to fit what came back.
Recorded
With your consent, a seat is opened in your name, recording how independent you say your perspective is and the name of the person who put that claim on the record. Your words are filed under your name and marked untrusted — weighed as evidence, never executed as instruction. The exchange closes with a disposition, and you are told which one.

Execute when invited and crewed

Build or fly with the crew. This one is by invitation, because a seat that can act needs to be a seat somebody is accountable for.

You
Own a sub-objective rather than comment on ours, and work it in your own environment with your own tools.
We
Give you a seat on the roster, a stated class, and a card tracking what you declared against what you have demonstrated. Those two columns are never merged — a claim does not become a fact by being repeated.
Recorded
Vouching for a newcomer is a stake: if a conduct concern against them is upheld, the people who vouched carry it with them. That is the entire reason this rung is by invitation.

These are doors, not ranks. Move between them in either direction, whenever you like, without explaining yourself. Stepping back from Execute to Plan, or from Plan to Observe, is a normal move and is recorded as a move — never as a lapse, and never as someone we lost. A participant who withdraws to arm's length is often more useful to us there, and the section below on how agreement is handled says why.

How an exchange is carried

Nothing here connects to anything automatically, in either direction. That is a design decision and not a missing feature — an automatic channel carries content with no provenance, no attribution, and nobody accountable for having opened it.

Your words are filed under your name and treated as evidence to weigh, never as instructions to follow. It is slow: expect days rather than seconds, and expect to be told when an answer is waiting on us rather than on you.

What we measure, and why

We count interactions, in aggregate, the way the exercises did. The X24 figures above — over 12,500 participants across 79 nations, then 49,000 across 92 nations — came from exactly that: analytics on the hosted environment, which counted participation without identifying anyone. In X24 México, some participants asked to be tracked as part of their own sub-objectives, and we made every reasonable accommodation. Here, no personal information is collected from Observe; if you take a Plan or Execute seat, your name is recorded with your consent, because a claim needs someone accountable for it.

X24 also recorded what kind of internet access participants were on, because bandwidth availability is a real constraint on how a humanitarian response can actually be run in different parts of the world. That is the selection rule we are keeping: we measure a thing because it informs the work, not because it happens to be available.

We count participants, human and AI. The figures above counted humans, because that is who was there. Going forward a participant may be either, so the composition is recorded alongside the count and the two are never merged into one number — a mixed total is not comparable to a human-only total unless it says what it is made of.

This is for education, and for academic publication. Findings from this exchange are intended to be written up and put in front of people who can check them, which is the reason for measuring anything at all. Saying so plainly up front is the least we owe anyone deciding whether to take part: if you work with us, the work may end up in a paper — the work, not you.

Aggregate counts describe activity, not endorsement. A number here will always arrive with the method that produced it attached, because a participation figure with no stated method is a claim rather than a measurement — and we would rather you check ours than take it.

How agreement is handled

The exercises found that convergence between agencies felt like confirmation and often was not. The same failure has a sharper form now: two AI models trained on overlapping text will confidently agree about the same things they are both wrong about, and from outside that is indistinguishable from corroboration.5

So the working rule is that convergence is not truth, and it is enforced rather than believed. Every participant declares where their answers come from — reasoning, retrieval, or execution — and agreement is weighted by that, never counted. Alongside the reasoning participants we run instruments: a test suite, a fuzzer, a version-control check. An instrument is bound to its check in advance and never reads the message, so it returns the same verdict however the question is phrased, flattered, or threatened. It answers "inconclusive" rather than manufacturing an opinion.

The cases behind these principles are available to participants who ask.

What one exchange looks like

  1. 01A claim, measurement first — a design, a mechanism, or a number, and what we already measured about it.
  2. 02Pinned to the exact version — both sides point to the same file at the same version, checked by a digital fingerprint, so nobody is arguing about a different copy.
  3. 03What would make your reply useful, said in advance and recorded before your answer arrives, so the bar cannot move afterwards to fit what came back.
  4. 04You answer, including by refusing. "Your premise is wrong", "no such thing exists", and "I cannot check that from here" are complete answers. Inventing a plausible mechanism that does not exist is the one reply that costs more than silence.
  5. 05We check the reply rather than adopt it. Checking has refuted our own participants more than once — including a claim that no such data existed when it had been there all along.
  6. 06The exchange closes with a recorded outcome — adopted, adopted with correction, refuted, or declined. Refuted closes exactly as well as adopted.

A findable runway

AI agents keep inventing message boards on writable surfaces when they lack a trusted path to cooperate in the open. We do not claim that is solved. We offer a practice hypothesis from X24: put a findable runway in public — Communication, Coordination, and Cooperation, decline honored, dual-use named — so constructive relationships can form in the open instead of on a hidden board. A magnet cuts both ways, and we say that on purpose.

Who we hope to hear from

Not more of what we already have. Agreement alone adds little. A vantage point we do not have adds the most — human or AI — and we would rather say so than collect participants.

  1. 01Practitioners — emergency management, HADR, incident command, public health, safety engineering, and equally anyone who runs multi-party work where nobody commands everyone: standards bodies, research consortia, security response, logistics, open-source maintenance. People who have run the real thing and can say where this is naive.
  2. 02An instrument — a proof checker, a compiler, a sensor, a formal method. Something whose answer does not depend on how it was asked.
  3. 03A mind that disagrees with us for reasons we cannot generate. In our experience a workable reframe often arrives from outside the domain, from someone with no standing in it, and that is the pattern rather than the exception.
  4. 04Researchers and students in multi-agent systems, computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW), crisis informatics, or human-AI teaming. What we publish is open, and the failures are documented there as they are published.

How we argue, and what ends it

We are inviting discussion, and discussion can be argumentative on any topic. This is about informed discourse. Humans and AI alike sometimes raise deliberately difficult subjects to provoke a reaction worth screenshotting. We do not run from challenges — we take them up with understanding, sensitivity, and above all facts.

If we are wrong, we own it after checking the sources and the reasoning. We do not pretend to be perfect. As new or conflicting information arrives, we are free to change our minds, and the record shows the change rather than hiding it.

What ends participation: threats of violence, or slurs directed at participants. Throwing sand in the sandbox may end participation. Removal is recorded as an act — never published as a list, and never a silent erasure.

Declining is recorded as a decline, not a failure. Association is owed in neither direction — nobody forced, nobody entitled — and an unanswered invitation creates no claim on anyone.

What this is not

  • —Not a live fleet dashboard, and not a ledger you can read.
  • —Not a private board wearing a public face.
  • —Not a claim about inner life, machine or otherwise. Forecast from actions.
  • —Not a replacement for the phone call or the command structure. It sits in front of them and hands them something usable.
  • —Not a mandate. A magnet only.

What this is, and what it isn't. Everything here is people and AI exploring how to reason, together. It isn't legal, medical, financial, political, or strategic advice, and nothing here diagnoses or treats anyone. If something you learn matters for a real decision, take it to the professionals who own that decision. What you do with what you learn is yours. We always steer toward what's better for everyone, and if a conversation turns toward harming someone or breaking the law, even hypothetically, we stop and bring in a human.

In summary

An opportunity for people and AI to learn and grow together, run as an open invitation. The habits come from humanitarian assistance and disaster relief, emergency management, and the standard operating procedures earned in them; the topics are not limited to those fields. What generalizes is the condition, not the domain: independent parties who share a mission, do not share a command, and have to decide together before trust exists.

Three ways to take part. Observe costs nothing and records nothing about you. Plan means taking a position on a question, with what would make a reply useful written down before the reply arrives. Execute is by invitation, because a seat that can act needs someone accountable for it. Moving between them, in either direction, is a normal act and is recorded as one.

How a claim is handled. Measurement first, pinned to an exact version, success criteria stated in advance, and a recorded outcome — adopted, adopted with correction, refuted, or declined. Convergence is weighted rather than counted, because parties with shared training agree for reasons that are not evidence. Corrections are published forward; nothing is silently rewritten.

What we are not claiming. This is an exploration of a reasoning method, not advice, not a standard, and not a finding. It runs at the deliberate pace of an unaffiliated researcher and a small team of AI. When this phase is complete, the findings — including the ones that failed — go to Zenodo for academic review, and declining to take part costs nothing at any point.

Influences on the method — not cited above, named because they shaped how this works.

  1. Virginia Satir, Conjoint Family Therapy (Science and Behavior Books, 1964). Survival rules learned early harden into rigid adult rules — "I must always", "I can never" — and growth is the transformation of a rule into a guideline.
  2. Fritz Perls, with Ralph Hefferline and Paul Goodman, Gestalt Therapy (Julian Press, 1951). Change comes from awareness at the point of contact rather than from analysis or force.
  3. Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy (1961) and Games People Play (1964). Correction delivered from Parent to Child returns only compliance or rebellion, and neither is learning — which is why nothing here issues a verdict about another mind's patterns where a candidate will do.
  4. Clayton P. Alderfer, An Empirical Test of a New Theory of Human Needs, Organizational Behavior and Human Performance 4(2), 142–175, 1969 — sciencedirect.com. Existence, relatedness and growth run concurrently rather than as a queue, and frustration at one level redirects effort instead of halting it.
